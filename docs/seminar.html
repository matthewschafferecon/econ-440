Part II

Advanced monetary economics

A modular second-half seminar connecting monetary theory, institutions, empirical evidence, and live policy questions.

Tuesdays and Thursdays · 12:30–1:45 p.m. · October 6–December 10

Advancement contingency.

If the team advances, selected seminar meetings retain their core reading and empirical application while reserving a final segment for College Fed Challenge Q&A preparation.

Provisional sequence

Eight modules

Each module will contain a core reading, optional extension, empirical application, discussion question, and short student-led component.

  1. Money demand, money creation, and liquidity

    What makes an asset money-like, how banks create deposits, and why liquidity demand changes under stress.

  2. The ample-reserves operating framework

    Administered rates, reserve demand, money-market control, and the central-bank balance sheet.

  3. Monetary transmission

    Banks, bond markets, household and firm balance sheets, asset prices, exchange rates, and expectations.

  4. Policy rules, discretion, and credibility

    Taylor-type rules, optimal policy, commitment, time inconsistency, and forward guidance.

  5. Financial stability and lender of last resort

    Liquidity versus solvency, fire sales, facilities, moral hazard, and the monetary-financial stability boundary.

  6. Heterogeneity and distribution

    Borrowers and savers, labor-market incidence, housing, wealth, heterogeneous-agent channels, and inclusion.

  7. International spillovers

    Exchange rates, global dollar funding, capital flows, policy synchronization, and emerging-market exposure.

  8. Monetary-fiscal interactions and independence

    Fiscal backing, debt maturity, remittances, dominance, legitimacy, accountability, and central-bank design.

Seminar method

A repeatable meeting structure

A stable structure makes it possible to compare very different monetary questions without reducing the course to reading summaries.

Before class

Analytical reading note

Question, mechanism, evidence, identifying assumption, and one objection.

In class

Mechanism and evidence

Reconstruct the model or institutional logic, then test it against data.

After class

Policy reflection

Explain what the material changes—or does not change—about a live policy judgment.

Reading standard

What to extract from a paper

Students should be able to move between the paper's formal or empirical structure and the policy claim without treating the abstract as a substitute for analysis.

Question
What uncertainty or disagreement is the paper designed to resolve?
Mechanism
Which behavior, friction, institution, or equilibrium condition produces the result?
Evidence
What data or model discipline the claim, and what variation identifies the effect?
Assumption
Which assumption is doing the most work, and how plausible is it for the policy setting?
External validity
Where, when, and for whom should the result apply?
Policy implication
What would a policymaker do differently if the result is correct?
